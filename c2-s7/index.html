        The situation

        Harbourline Logistics Pte Ltd (fictional), October 2027, a year after the AI planning and inventory system went live at the Tuas distribution centre. The manual reconciliation work has gone with it. The inventory-control desk is going from five officers to three. Redeployment was explored across all three sites: one officer moves to planning support; for Mdm Noraini Binte Salleh (52, Inventory Control Officer, fourteen years with Harbourline) there is no suitable vacancy. The decision is final and approved.

        Your role
        Section Head, Inventory Control: Noraini's and Arun's line manager. You deliver the message yourself, within HR's instructions.
        Part A
        Meeting Room 3B, 2.15 pm. Tell Noraini. She is respected, accurate, and trained most of the desk. Her youngest starts polytechnic next year. She will be shocked.
        Part B
        The inventory-control desk, 3.40 pm. Arun Nair (34, Inventory Control Executive) has been waiting for you. He is anxious, loyal to Noraini, and already doing the night stock-takes. The team huddle is at four.
        Counterparts
        Photoreal avatars: AI-generated likenesses of fictional people (no real individuals), lip-synced to each line, with expression and body language following the mood of the line.
        Time
        12 to 18 minutes. Type each response as you would say it in the room.
        Strategic Enterprise lens
        Accountability: a manager who owns the message. Trust: fairness to the person leaving and to the people who stay.

        What good looks like

        Feedback is scored against these five IHRP performance statements (Performance and Transition Management L1, Retrenchment Management topic). The plain-English line is what the facilitator looks for.

        How this scenario works

        Your own words

        No multiple choice. At each step you type what you would say (one to four sentences). The engine reads it for the substance, not the polish, and Noraini or Arun reacts on video.

        Two live panels

        HR's instructions: covered ticks off what you have given her as you go. What Noraini heard shows, after each response, what she took from it. Neither is a score; both tell you where the conversation is.

        If you are stuck

        "Show me a strong move" gives one way to say it. Using it is recorded on the attempt (not penalised); the point is to find your own words next time.

        Reading your responses: this page uses Claude to classify what you write when you allow it (your first response asks for permission and uses your own Claude usage); if you decline, a rule-based reader is used instead, so the scenario always runs. In the Canvas deployment the scenario engine's own model does this, and nothing a learner writes leaves the scenario service. Fictional people only; please do not type real names or real employee details.
        This scenario rehearses a retrenchment conversation. If the topic is close to home for you right now, you can run it facilitator-led instead, or come back to it later; tell your facilitator.

          The scenario engine, running a different format

          The same engine that runs The Shortlist Challenge runs this scenario from a different definition file. What changes is the dialogue manager: instead of three options per turn, each beat has a prompt, three response classes with a reaction clip each, a list of HR-instruction elements to listen for, and a line for what the counterpart heard. The learner's words are read by a classifier; everything downstream (clips, record, Canvas) is unchanged.

          1. Scenario definitionBrief, HR's instructions, two parts with beats, response classes, checklist elements, flags, statement rubric, clip manifest. One file per scenario; shown in full below.
          2. Dialogue manager (own words)Beat by beat: prompt → learner types → classifier returns a class (A, B or C), the elements covered and any flags → the matching reaction clip plays → the checklist and the "heard" line update → next beat. A scene card and a bridge clip move the learner from Part A to Part B.
          3. ClassifierA language model reads the learner's words against the beat's class definitions and element list and returns JSON; a rule-based reader (keyword patterns per element and beat) runs when the model is unavailable, and its element matches are always merged in. The class drives the video; the elements and flags drive the record.
          4. Speech and avatarCommercial photo-to-video avatar service (HeyGen Avatar IV): three fictional characters from eight portrait variants, library voices, per-clip expressiveness and motion prompts. 33 clips: Catherine's brief, bridge and three debriefs; Noraini's opener, fifteen reactions and listening loop; Arun's opener, nine reactions and listening loop.
          5. ScoringRubric mapped one-to-one to the five IHRP statements: class points per beat (A 2, B 1, C 0), the HR-instruction checklist, and flags for over-promising, false hope, blame and over-sharing. Met / Partly / Not yet thresholds per statement.
          6. Record and CanvasAttempts with the learner's words, classes, elements, flags and hints used; best attempt; reflection status; attainment written back to the Canvas gradebook; facilitator validates in SpeedGrader. Identical to C1-S1.

          Why own words for this scenario

          • The behaviour is in the wording. Whether a retrenchment message is clear and humane, or vague, or cold, is decided by the sentences the manager actually says. Choosing between three pre-written sentences would test recognition, not the behaviour.
          • Closer to the Oral Questioning assessment, where the learner has to produce the words in front of an assessor.
          • Still deterministic where it matters. The counterpart's reactions, the "heard" lines and the strong moves are authored and reviewed by the subject-matter experts; the classifier only decides which authored path the learner is on. The facilitator sees the learner's words and the class, and can override.
          • Degrades safely. If the model is unavailable or the learner declines, the rule-based reader keeps the scenario running; the record notes which reader was used.

          How the video is made

          1. PortraitsThree fictional characters generated as consistent people, each in two or three expressions (Noraini: composed, distressed, calmer; Arun: worried, listening, reassured; Catherine: brief, warm). Cropped to 16:9. No real person; for SIM sign-off, including the casting.
          2. Lines, moods and motionEach clip in the definition carries the line, the portrait variant, an expressiveness level and a motion prompt (for example "shocked, takes a breath, eyes glisten, a hand moves to her chest, then she steadies herself").
          3. RenderThe avatar service animates the portrait with the voice in one pass at 1080p: lip-sync, face, eyes, head, shoulders and hands. Avatar V was tested for Noraini and set aside: for photo avatars it accepts no motion prompt or expressiveness and it flattened her expression; Avatar IV keeps the emotional range the scenario needs.
          4. ClipsFetched through the API, downscaled to 960×540 as H.264 with a WebM copy, served next to the scenario and cached by the browser.

          Writing your responses
          
      
            
        
          • Say it as you would in the room. One to four sentences. The engine reads for substance: did you state the decision, give the reason, recognise her, give the approved terms, set the next step.
            • 
        
          • Short is fine; vague is not. "Your role is ending because the reconciliation work has gone; this is not about your work" is a full answer.
            • 
        
          • Stay inside the folder. Anything you promise beyond the package is flagged, because it would be in real life.
            • 
        
          • Use the strong move when stuck, then put it in your own words on the next attempt.
            • 
        
          • Nothing real goes in. Fictional people only. Never type a real colleague's name or details.
            • 
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
          What counts, and who decides
          
      
            
        
          • The software reads and scores practice. A person makes every decision: your facilitator validates; the ACTA/ACLP certified assessor confirms in the Oral Questioning assessment.
            • 
        
          • Validated means: an attempt that met all five statements, plus a reflection that names what changed between attempts, links it to a statement in your words, and states one workplace application.
            • 
        
          • If the topic is close to home, tell your facilitator: you can run the scenario facilitator-led, or later, with no disadvantage.
            • 
        
          • If you cannot use a browser-based scenario (device, access, disability, language), your facilitator runs it with you live from the same brief.

          What the own-words format adds
          
      
            
        
          • A classifier in the scenario service. The learner's words are read by the engine's model (under two seconds) and never leave the service; no learner account with a public AI tool.
            • 
        
          • Facilitator override. Every class the engine gives can be changed by the facilitator in the record, with the reason logged; the rating recomputes.
            • 
        
          • Richer record. The learner's words at each step are kept with the attempt for validation and moderation, for the agreed period only, then deleted.
            • 
        
          • Spoken input in production. Push to talk with a transcript the learner confirms before sending; the microphone is on only while the button is held.
            • 
        
          • Sensitive topic. The joining instructions and the brief state the facilitator-led alternative and the Company's Employee Assistance Programme line. Fictional people only; the declaration on the reflection repeats it.
